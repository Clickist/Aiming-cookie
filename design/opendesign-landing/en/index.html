Done — and your weakness is actually obvious; it's hiding in that "half a beat slow" of yours. Your latest run is 1wall 6targets small: 123 flicks in total, averaging 470 ms of movement per flick. Only the first 157 ms is spent accelerating to top speed; the remaining 308 ms — a full two-thirds of the time — is all deceleration. And peak speed arrives just a third of the way through the movement, meaning you slow down right after the flick starts and spend the rest crawling onto the target. Worse, peak speed dropped to 32,557 this time — the lowest of your four recorded runs on this scenario (the previous three were 46,486, 41,036 and 43,174), roughly 25% slower. So the "half a beat slow" you feel isn't slow reactions; your motion pattern has changed: the initial drive has lost its punch, and the finish drags.

Scroll back and verify it yourself. The opening flick is the clearest case: @00:00 watch it spend nearly ninety percent of its time decelerating, the crosshair crawling onto the target; @00:02.1 check whether the main flick and the confirm click come out as two separate pieces; @00:14.3 watch whether the crosshair shivers backward near the target before you click. Then compare @00:04.8 — that flick ends much cleaner, and you can feel the difference between the two finishes right away. One question before I draw any conclusion, though: when you've been running this scenario lately, have you been deliberately slowing down for consistency? If it's intentional, this pattern is what you chose — not a problem; if not, then we can start talking about a dragging finish and a weak drive.

My advice is one mantra: one flick, one brake, click when it stops. Forget flick speed for now and train only the deceleration — glide the mouse from top speed down onto the target in one even pull, and click only once it has settled.

Frequently asked questions

Is Aiming Cookie an aimbot?

No. It doesn't read game memory, doesn't inject, and never aims for you — it works outside the game: recording mouse raw input, screen capture, KovaaK's own stats and engine telemetry, then analyzing after you're done training. If you searched for "AI aim assist", rest assured: this is a different thing entirely.

Is Aiming Cookie free?

Yes. Sign up and verify for free — one full training analysis plus two follow-up questions, so you can confirm Aiming Cookie runs well on your PC first. After that, subscribe (model calls included) or keep using your own API key for free.

Do I need to own KovaaK's?

Yes. Aiming Cookie is the analysis and coaching layer for KovaaK's (FPSAimTrainer on Steam); the training itself still happens in KovaaK's.

Does my data get uploaded?

Training data — mouse input, screen recordings, analysis results — stays on your own PC. Data only leaves in two cases: you actively chat with the AI coach (sending the content you select), or you actively upload logs to report a problem.

How is this better than reviewing my demos?

Demo review is guessing from pixels. Aiming Cookie computes: true angular error per shot (measured median 0.2–0.5° on kill clicks), deceleration share, path smoothness — off-screen targets included. None of that is visible in footage.

Which training types are supported?

Static / dynamic clicking, continuous tracking, target switching — the mainstream KovaaK's scenario categories.

What can the AI coach help with?

Explaining weaknesses, building training plans, reading re-tests, gear talk (based on Gearclickist's experience, only when you ask), and it can even open the KovaaK's scenario you need to train.

What are the system requirements?

Windows 10/11 64-bit with KovaaK's installed. Analysis runs on engine telemetry instead of vision models, so it doesn't eat your GPU.

Note: subscriptions include model calls; you can also bring your own API key and use it for free.
